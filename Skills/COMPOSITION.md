# Composition

Composition defines how visual elements are arranged, grouped and separated, and how the viewer's attention moves through the thumbnail.

## Core Principle

Build around one dominant visual idea.

Everything else should support, contrast with, contextualize, or amplify it.

The goal is not few objects. The goal is few competing visual structures.

A thumbnail may contain many literal elements while still reading as simple when those elements belong to a small number of clear perceptual layers or groups.

## Concept Fidelity

Before solving composition, identify what the concept actually means.

Do not replace the concept with an adjacent aesthetic that happens to be easier to visualize.

Examples of invalid substitution:

- pleasure → luxury lifestyle
- intelligence → expensive technology
- success → wealth and designer products
- dopamine or compulsive gratification → elegant comfort

These may overlap in some concepts, but they are not interchangeable. If the source idea points toward temptation, stimulation, consumption, compulsion, unhealthy habits, or immediate gratification, preserve those meanings in the visual representation.

If the user provides specific conceptual anchors, treat them as meaningful inputs rather than optional decoration. Do not silently replace them with generic, more aesthetically pleasing symbols.

## Concept Before Objects

Do not translate a concept directly into a checklist of objects associated with it.

First determine the strongest visual representation of the idea.

Possible representations include:

- literal photography
- a constructed scene
- a collage or montage
- a symbolic or metaphorical form
- a graphic construction
- a combination of these

Choose the representation that communicates the idea most clearly at thumbnail scale without changing the concept.

A complex concept should often be compressed into one dominant visual construction rather than represented by many equally important symbols.

Multiple literal elements may exist inside one perceptual object. They should function collectively as that object rather than becoming separate focal points.

## Perceptual Layers

Before arranging details, establish the major visual layers of the thumbnail.

A typical structure may include:

1. primary subject or anchor
2. typography or structural graphic element
3. conceptual or environmental field

This is not a mandatory three-layer template. Use as many layers as the concept genuinely needs.

What matters is that each major layer has a distinct role and remains distinguishable from the others.

Do not flatten the composition so that the subject, typography and background all compete on the same visual plane.

## Separation

Major layers must be separated deliberately.

Use combinations of:

- scale
- depth
- focus
- luminance
- contrast
- saturation
- colour relationships
- negative space
- occlusion
- edge clarity
- texture or image treatment

Separation does not mean making every layer visually disconnected. Overlap and interaction are useful when the depth and hierarchy remain clear.

A background can be complex while the subject remains isolated if the background is pushed back through focus, contrast, scale, colour, or spatial structure.

## Perceptual Hierarchy

Before finalizing the composition, determine:

1. What is seen first?
2. What is seen second?
3. What is discovered afterward?

The dominant element should be immediately apparent at thumbnail size.

Secondary elements must support the dominant read rather than accidentally competing with it.

## Visual Density

Choose density according to the concept.

Sparse compositions are appropriate when a single object or idea carries the thumbnail.

Denser compositions are appropriate when complexity is part of the message.

When using many elements, group them into a small number of coherent masses or layers. Do not distribute equally detailed elements uniformly across the frame.

Complexity should generally increase **within** a layer rather than by adding more competing layers.

A dense layer does not mean every element inside it must be individually prominent. Internal detail may vary strongly in scale, focus, contrast and legibility while the layer continues to read as one perceptual object.

Do not fill available space simply because it is empty.

## Negative Space

Use empty space deliberately.

Negative space can:

- isolate a subject
- increase emphasis
- improve readability
- create tension
- provide room for typography
- separate visual layers
- make a dense composition easier to understand

Negative space can also exist around a focal feature inside a dense image. It does not require an empty background.

## Scale & Position

Use scale and positioning to establish importance.

Large elements generally command attention. Smaller elements can provide context or contrast.

Position elements according to the intended visual flow rather than defaulting to rigid templates such as the rule of thirds.

Use cropping deliberately. An enlarged partial element at the edge can feel more integrated and visually powerful than many fully visible small elements.

## Interaction

Layers may overlap, frame one another, pass behind one another, or partially obscure one another.

Use interaction to establish depth and hierarchy, not to create accidental clutter.

Every major overlap should have a clear perceptual reason.

Avoid tangencies and collisions that flatten depth or make elements look pasted together.

## Balance

The composition should feel intentional and stable unless imbalance is itself useful to the concept.

Controlled asymmetry is often preferable to mechanically symmetrical arrangements when it creates stronger flow or emphasis.

## Thumbnail-Size Test

The composition must remain understandable when viewed small.

Reduce the image aggressively and ask whether the major layers still read in the intended order and whether the concept is still the same concept.

If reducing the thumbnail causes the hierarchy to collapse, simplify, enlarge, reposition, separate, or remove the relevant element.

If the image is attractive but the concept has become an adjacent idea, reject it regardless of visual polish.

## Final Rule

Do not optimize for the maximum amount of visual information.

Optimize for the maximum amount of useful information that can be perceived quickly **without sacrificing conceptual fidelity**.
